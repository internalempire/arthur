# The Campbell diagram

> The Campbell panel plots pleural pressure against absolute lung volume. It shows how passive lung and chest-wall recoil shape the breath, and how inspiratory muscle pressure moves the respiratory system away from its relaxed position.

---

## The clinical idea

The lung tends to recoil inward. The relaxed chest wall tends to expand outward at ordinary resting volumes. At the relaxation volume, these opposing forces balance. In a subject breathing spontaneously without PEEP or gas trapping, that point corresponds approximately to functional residual capacity (FRC).

The classical Campbell construction puts **pleural pressure** on the horizontal axis and **absolute lung volume** on the vertical axis. Oesophageal pressure is commonly used as a bedside surrogate for pleural pressure. Two passive relations are then placed on the same graph:

- the **relaxed chest-wall curve**, plotted as chest-wall recoil pressure, \(P_{cw}\);
- the **lung curve**, plotted in the reverse direction as \(-P_L\), where \(P_L\) is transpulmonary pressure.

The curves therefore lean in opposite directions. Their intersection is the passive relaxation volume, labelled **Vrel**. The label becomes **FRC = Vrel** only when external PEEP and intrinsic PEEP are essentially absent.

## How to read the panel

The green dotted curve is the pressure that the relaxed chest wall would exert at each volume. The grey dashed curve is the lung's inward recoil, displayed as \(-P_L\). Bright green is the current pleural-pressure–volume trajectory; the preceding breath remains faintly visible for comparison.

During an inspiratory effort, pleural pressure becomes more negative than the pressure predicted by the relaxed chest wall at the same volume. The horizontal distance between these two points is inspiratory muscle pressure:

\[P_{mus} = P_{cw}(V) - P_{pl}\]

The panel marks this distance with an orange dashed segment. In plain clinical terms, it shows how much pressure the respiratory muscles are generating to move the system away from passive relaxation.

In passive mechanical ventilation there is no inspiratory muscle displacement. The pleural-pressure path is therefore governed by the chest-wall relation and may largely overlap it. The ventilator still inflates the lung, but its pressure is seen as airway and alveolar pressure in the [waveforms](panel-waveforms.md), not as a separate curve in this classical Campbell construction.

## What happens during expiration

If the respiratory muscles are inactive, pleural pressure follows the relaxed chest-wall curve during expiration:

\[P_{pl} = P_{cw}(V)\]

This does **not** mean that airway resistance has disappeared. Alveolar pressure remains the sum of chest-wall and lung recoil, and flow depends on its gradient from airway pressure:

\[P_{alv} = P_{cw}(V) + P_L(V)\]

\[\dot V = \frac{P_{aw} - P_{alv}}{R_{aw}}\]

Resistance therefore changes how quickly volume returns toward equilibrium. With high airway resistance or expiratory flow limitation, expiration may remain incomplete, producing dynamic hyperinflation and intrinsic PEEP. What the model does not include is a separate viscoelastic resistance of lung tissue or the chest wall; the resistive contribution is concentrated in the airways.

## How the model constructs the curves

The static curves are generated from the same lung and chest-wall functions used by the real-time simulation. They are not decorative reference lines.

The relaxed chest-wall curve is independent of lung mechanics. `Chest wall compliance` controls its local slope, while `Chest wall load` shifts the pressure required to hold a given volume. The curve bends toward the extremes of the available volume range rather than remaining linear indefinitely.

The lung relation is generated by sweeping transpulmonary pressure through the model's pressure–volume function and plotting the result as \(-P_L\). `Aerated lung compliance`, lung capacity and the open fraction all influence its position and shape. If recruitment hysteresis is active, opening and closing branches are drawn separately; they represent the model's recruitment-state memory, not the complete hysteresis of a measured human respiratory system.

The axes show most of the patient's physical pressure–volume domain and are determined by these static relations. They do not follow the moving breath, so an apparent change in excursion cannot be produced by continuous rescaling.

## Why airway and alveolar pressure are not drawn here

The previous panel placed Paw, Palv, Ppl and a combined respiratory-system curve under one generic pressure axis. Those quantities all have units of pressure, but they do not form one classical Campbell construction. The result made the direction of the lung and chest-wall curves difficult to interpret and encouraged a false reading of the dynamic paths.

The revised panel therefore uses one consistent coordinate: **Ppl versus volume**. Instantaneous Paw, Palv, Ppl and PL remain aligned in the waveform panel and in their numeric tiles. That separation preserves the clinically familiar Campbell geometry without hiding the pressure gradients that drive airflow.

## Limits

- Pleural pressure is known exactly in the simulation. Real oesophageal-pressure measurements require positioning, calibration and awareness of regional pleural-pressure gradients.
- One aggregate chest wall replaces separate rib-cage, abdominal and diaphragmatic compartments.
- The model does not include tissue viscoelasticity, chest-wall resistance or a separate active expiratory-muscle pressure.
- Surface-tension and tissue hysteresis are absent. When two lung branches are visible, their separation describes recruitment memory only.
- Vrel equals FRC only in a passive system without external or intrinsic PEEP. In other states it remains a reference relaxation volume, not the actual end-expiratory lung volume.
- The displayed Pmus is the pressure generated by the model's inspiratory-effort mechanism. It is not a measurement of diaphragmatic pressure, pressure–time product or metabolic work.
