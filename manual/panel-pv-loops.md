# Ventricular pressure–volume loops

> The paired RV and LV loops show how one beat changes with filling, contractility and load, using transmural rather than atmospheric chamber pressure.

---

## Physiology

A pressure–volume loop follows ventricular filling, isovolumic contraction, ejection and isovolumic relaxation. Its width is stroke volume; its end-diastolic and end-systolic positions show how volume is partitioned. The end-systolic pressure–volume relation (ESPVR) expresses contractility, while the end-diastolic relation expresses passive stiffness.

## How to read the panel

The RV is on the left and the LV on the right. The current beat is bright; the preceding completed beat is faded, making a recent intervention visible without accumulating an unreadable trail.

The dashed straight line is the local effective ESPVR for the completed beat. It passes through the model's zero-pressure volume and the end-systolic point on the loop. The curved dashed line is labelled EDPVR: the end-diastolic pressure–volume relation. There is no separate point marker because end systole is already the upper-left corner of each completed loop.

The dotted line labelled $E_a$ joins **end-diastolic volume at zero pressure**, $(EDV,0)$, to the end-systolic point, $(ESV,P_{es})$. Its slope magnitude is therefore $P_{es}/(EDV-ESV)=P_{es}/SV$. It does not start at ESV on the volume axis. Volume is on the horizontal axis and transmural pressure on the vertical axis. The title reports loop width in mL.

The axes remain fixed while the same parameter settings are running. Each panel starts with a clinically useful RV- or LV-specific range and expands only when a loop would otherwise leave it. It does not repeatedly contract around the moving beat. Changing a control or scenario allows the panel to fit the new state again.

When the simulation is paused and the waveform time cursor is moved, a small neutral point identifies the ventricular pressure and volume at the selected instant. This cursor marker is not an end-systolic marker and disappears when live playback resumes.

Read both ventricles together. A pulmonary load increase may shift the RV toward larger volume and smaller stroke volume; the LV response can appear later because changing RV output crosses the [pulmonary circulation](pulmonary-transit.md) before altering LV filling.

## In the model

Loop pressure is transmural. Pleural and pericardial pressure are removed from the pressure experienced by myocardium, so a uniform change in surrounding pressure does not falsely appear as greater ventricular work. End-systolic pressure is also stored transmural.

The `eesRv` and `eesLv` controls set the intrinsic active elastance used inside each ventricular pressure equation. Total end-systolic chamber pressure can also contain ventricular-interdependence terms. This matters especially for the RV, because part of its systolic pressure is generated by LV and septal contraction. A line drawn only with `eesRv` could therefore lie below the actual RV end-systolic point even though both numbers came from the same beat.

The displayed local ESPVR avoids that contradiction. It keeps the model's zero-pressure volume and uses the slope required to pass through the actual end-systolic point:

$$
E_{es,\,effective}=\frac{P_{es}}{ESV-V_0}
$$

This is a single-beat visual summary of the **total chamber behaviour**. It does not replace the selected intrinsic elastance, and it is not equivalent to measuring a full ESPVR from several loops obtained during preload reduction.

The displayed $E_a$ line uses the end-systolic model point, but it remains a single-beat graphical surrogate. The application does not publish $E_{es}/E_a$ as a validated number; see [ventriculo-arterial coupling](ventriculo-arterial-coupling.md).

## Limits

- Loops come from lumped elastance chambers, not conductance-catheter measurements.
- The displayed effective ESPVR is anchored to one beat. A physiological ESPVR is more rigorously obtained from a family of end-systolic points under different loading conditions.
- There is no measurement noise, parallel conductance, pressure calibration error or single-beat estimation uncertainty.
- Valves are ideal one-way resistances: no stenosis, regurgitation or prosthetic behaviour.
- Pressure–volume area must not be interpreted as myocardial oxygen consumption because coronary flow and energetics are absent.
- The RV loop does not represent regional geometry or free-wall versus septal work.
